215|||Unlike land engines, marine engines run under constant humidity, salt and varying load. Regular maintenance both reduces the risk of failure and extends the life of the engine. The list below gives a general framework. For exact intervals, the engine manufacturer's manual should be your reference.
216|||What to do every season
217|||Changing the engine oil and oil filter.
218|||Checking and changing the fuel filter and water separator.
219|||Checking the raw-water pump impeller. A torn impeller can end cooling in an instant.
220|||Checking belt tension and cracks.
221|||Checking the coolant level and the antifreeze ratio.
222|||Checking hoses, clamps and connections.
223|||Easily overlooked details
224|||Sacrificial anodes, the exhaust elbow and the heat exchanger wear quietly. These parts are often not checked until something fails. A short visual check at the start of the season can prevent an expensive intervention mid-season.
225|||Winterising and long periods of non-use
226|||If the boat will not be used for a long time, the fuel system and the cooling circuit need protecting. Flushing the engine with fresh water, changing the oil and keeping the battery on charge are the basic steps.
227|||When to call a specialist
228|||If the engine produces more smoke than expected.
229|||If the temperature gauge reads higher than normal.
230|||If the oil pressure is dropping or fluctuating.
231|||If there is an unusual noise or vibration.
232|||If you notice any of these signs, stop the engine without forcing it and tell the technical service. Early intervention often prevents damage that would otherwise need an overhaul.

236|||A used boat can hide expensive surprises in the engine room even if it looks clean from the outside. If someone independent checks the engine and electrical systems before you decide to buy, you both strengthen your negotiating position and protect yourself from unexpected costs.
237|||What to check on the engine
238|||Compression test: The difference between cylinders is the clearest clue to the engine's internal health.
239|||Cold start: Note it down if the engine struggles on the first start, smokes or runs unevenly.
240|||Exhaust colour: Bluish smoke can point to oil burning, white smoke to a coolant leak, and black smoke to a fuel or air imbalance.
241|||Oil and coolant: If the oil looks milky or there are traces of oil in the coolant, a deeper inspection is needed.
242|||Shaft and propeller line: Vibration, noise and water leakage points should be checked.
243|||Running hours: Do the hour meter and the maintenance records agree with each other?
244|||What to check on the electrical system
245|||Battery bank: Batteries should be tested under load. A voltage reading alone is not enough.
246|||Charging system: Do the alternator, the charger and the shore power connection work correctly?
247|||Panel and wiring: Corrosion, signs of overheating, non-standard splices and careless connections are warning signs of faults.
248|||Additional equipment: Look at the connection quality of the inverter, the generator and any devices added later.
249|||Why is the report important?
250|||A written report with photos records every finding that was seen. That way you have concrete data when discussing the sale price. If the report separates urgent, near-term and monitor-only findings, you can plan your budget correctly.
251|||Hull and bottom surveys (structural surveys) are a separate field of expertise and are outside our scope. We assess the technical condition of the engine, mechanical and electrical systems.
252|||Metals in water are in an electrochemical relationship. When different metals such as the propeller, shaft, rudder and engine are together, the more active metal starts to corrode. A sacrificial anode is deliberately made from a more active metal, and it takes on this corrosion to protect the valuable parts.
253|||Which anode for which water?
254|||Salt water: Zinc or aluminium anodes are used.
255|||Fresh water (lake, dam): Magnesium anodes are more suitable. Zinc can lose its effectiveness over time in fresh water.
256|||Brackish water (river mouth): Aluminium is usually preferred.
257|||The water your boat sits in is the first criterion for choosing the right anode. If the same boat moves from fresh water to salt water, the anode choice should be reviewed as well.
258|||When should it be replaced?
259|||An anode is due for replacement when about half of it has dissolved. An anode with a crusted surface, or one that is painted or heavily worn, may also not be doing its job. It should be checked at least once a year, when the boat is hauled out. On heavily used boats it may need checking more often.
260|||Common mistakes
261|||Painting over the anode. Paint makes the anode useless.
262|||Fitting the anode loosely or with poor contact. A good electrical connection is essential.
263|||Looking only at the anode and not checking the bonding lines.
264|||Choosing the wrong material for the water.
265|||A worn anode shows that the system is working. The real problem is an anode that never wears, or unexpected signs of corrosion on the parts. In that case the bonding and grounding lines should be inspected.
266|||NMEA 2000 is the standard that lets a boat's navigation and engine devices communicate over a single shared network. Instead of wiring each device separately, all of them connect to a backbone. This reduces cable clutter and makes adding new devices easier.
267|||The basic parts of the network
268|||Backbone: The shared cable that runs the length of the boat.
269|||T-connectors: The points where devices connect to the backbone.
270|||Terminators: There is one at each end of the backbone.
271|||Power supply: The network is supplied with 12 volts from a single point.
272|||Leaving out a terminator, or using more than two.
273|||Making the device connection cables (drops) unnecessarily long.
274|||Powering the network from more than one point.
275|||Not checking compatibility between devices from different brands beforehand.
276|||The symptoms of these mistakes are often gauges that work intermittently, data that disappears, or devices that cannot see each other. The problem is usually not in the device, but in how the network was installed.
277|||Why is calibration important?
278|||The compass, autopilot and speed sensors must be calibrated after installation. An uncalibrated system displays wrong data even when it works correctly. Installing and calibrating together matters for safe navigation.
279|||Most brands such as Garmin, Raymarine, Simrad and Furuno work with NMEA 2000. Checking whether a new device is compatible with your existing network before you buy it reduces problems later.
280|||Most electrical faults on a boat start with the battery. A weak battery can cause the engine not to start, devices to switch off on their own and misleading fault symptoms. That is why battery care, however simple it seems, is the foundation of the system.
281|||The difference between a starter battery and a service battery
282|||A starter battery delivers a high current for a short time to start the engine. A service (deep-cycle) battery feeds loads such as lights, the refrigerator and electronic devices over a long period. Mixing the two shortens their life. If possible, two separate battery groups should be used.
283|||Voltage alone is not enough
284|||A rested, fully charged lead-acid battery reads about 12.6 to 12.8 volts. Around 12 volts indicates a serious discharge. However, voltage does not show how the battery will behave under load. A battery that looks healthy can collapse quickly once a load is applied. That is why a load test, or a capacity check with a testing device, is needed.
285|||Basic maintenance steps
286|||Keep the terminals clean and tight. White or green deposits create resistance.
287|||Check the battery hold-downs and the ventilation.
288|||Make sure the charger is set to the profile that suits the battery type.
289|||If the boat will not be used for a long time, disconnect the battery at the main switch or keep it on a maintenance charge.
290|||Do not combine batteries of different age and type in the same bank.
291|||When should a battery be replaced?
292|||It is time to replace it if it drains quickly despite charging, fails the load test, or has swelling or leaks in its case. Age and usage pattern matter as well.
293|||If you want to enlarge the battery bank or add an inverter, the cable sizes, fuses and charging arrangement should be planned together. In marine electrical work these parts are assessed as a whole, not one by one.

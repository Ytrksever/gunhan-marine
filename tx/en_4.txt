516|||Antifouling choice depends on the water, the hull material and how the boat is used. Copper-based paint on an aluminium hull carries a galvanic corrosion risk. A strong ablative paint suits heavy marine growth in the warm Mediterranean, while a lighter system is often enough in a lake.
517|||Antifouling is a paint that slows the growth of algae, mussels and other organisms on the hull. The same paint does not give the same result in every water or on every boat. In this article we summarise the main points to consider when choosing for a lake and for the Mediterranean.
518|||The difference between a lake and the Mediterranean
519|||Fresh water (lake, reservoir): Growth is mostly algae and biofilm. A lighter system is often enough.
520|||Mediterranean: Warm water, heavy growth all summer and shelled organisms (mussels, barnacles) call for strong protection.
521|||Usage frequency: A boat that sails often renews the paint surface by itself; a boat that stays moored for long needs a stronger system.
522|||Hull material and paint type
523|||Copper-based antifouling is widely used on polyester (fibreglass) and wooden hulls. On an aluminium hull, copper-based paint can accelerate galvanic corrosion; for these hulls a copper-free system and a suitable barrier primer should be chosen.
524|||Ablative (self-eroding) paints wear slowly from the surface through the season, exposing a new layer. Hard (matrix) paints do not wear away and are preferred on faster boats.
525|||Application mistakes
526|||Not checking whether the old paint and the new paint are compatible.
527|||Painting without sanding and cleaning the surface enough.
528|||Covering over without checking for osmosis or damage.
529|||Painting parts such as the propeller, shaft and anodes wrongly. Never paint over an anode.
530|||Paint consumption and season length vary with usage and water. If you are not sure which paint suits your boat, a short check before application protects your budget.

544|||The most common causes of marine engine overheating are a blocked seawater intake, a worn impeller, a stuck thermostat, a dirty heat exchanger, a loose belt, a clogged exhaust elbow, and air or a shortage in the coolant. If the temperature rises, stop the engine immediately.
545|||When the temperature gauge rises, the first reaction should be to stop the engine. Running an overheated engine can lead to costly consequences up to cylinder head and gasket damage. The list below summarises the seven most common causes in order.
546|||Seven possible causes
547|||Seawater intake: A closed seacock, plastic, weed or a blocked strainer stops water reaching the engine.
548|||Impeller: A worn, cracked or vane-less impeller cannot pump enough water.
549|||Thermostat: A thermostat that does not open stops coolant circulation.
550|||Heat exchanger and oil cooler: Salt, scale and shell deposits reduce heat transfer.
551|||Belt and fresh water pump: A loose or broken belt cannot turn the pump.
552|||Exhaust elbow: An elbow narrowed inside by carbon and salt restricts exhaust and cooling flow.
553|||Coolant: In a closed circuit, if the level is low or there is air in the circuit, the temperature rises quickly.
554|||First checks
555|||Stop the engine and let it cool for about fifteen minutes. Do not open the expansion tank cap while hot.
556|||Check that the seacock is open and the intake strainer is clean.
557|||On outboards, check that the tell-tale (cooling water) stream is present.
558|||Check the belt tension and the coolant level.
559|||An impeller can wear faster in muddy or sandy water. In hot months the seawater is also warmer, so the cooling system has less margin; even a small blockage shows symptoms faster in summer.
560|||If you cannot find the problem or the engine overheats again, do not strain the engine. Early action often prevents cylinder head and gasket damage.
561|||Should I stop the engine immediately when it overheats?
562|||Yes. When the temperature gauge rises, stop the engine without straining it and let it cool. Continuing can cause cylinder head and gasket damage.
563|||Does the engine overheat right away when the impeller tears?
564|||Most of the time, yes. If the seawater pump impeller tears, the cooling water flow drops suddenly and the temperature rises quickly.

594|||Cable size is chosen by current, cable length and the permitted voltage drop. In a 12-volt system the voltage drop is limited to about 3% for navigation lights and electronics and about 10% for other loads. On a boat, tinned multistrand copper cable and lugs fitted with a proper crimping tool are used.
595|||A cable that heats up on a boat is often the result of a wrongly chosen size or a badly crimped lug. A thin cable seems to carry the current but drops the voltage, heats up and makes devices misbehave. The right size and a solid connection are the cheapest precaution in marine electrics.
596|||Three values that set the size
597|||Current: The amps the load draws. The fuse and the cable are chosen by this value.
598|||Cable length: The total of the outgoing and return runs is counted, not only the outgoing one.
599|||Permitted voltage drop: About 3% for navigation lights and electronics and about 10% for other loads is accepted.
600|||Practical calculation: Size (mm²) ≈ 2 × length (m) × current (A) × 0.0172 ÷ permitted voltage drop (V). For example, for a load drawing 10 amps, 6 metres away in a 12-volt system, the 3% limit is 0.36 volts. The calculation gives about 5.7 mm², so the next standard size, 6 mm², is chosen.
601|||Why tinned and multistrand cable?
602|||In a salty, humid environment bare copper tarnishes, its resistance rises and green corrosion creeps in from the strand ends. Tinned copper resists this far better. Flexible multistrand cable withstands vibration; solid household cable breaks over time on a boat.
603|||Lug and crimping mistakes
604|||Connecting the cable with a lug that does not match its size.
605|||Crushing the lug with pliers or a hammer. A hexagonal or indent crimping tool should be used.
606|||Making the connection with solder only. A soldered joint breaks under vibration.
607|||Leaving the connection exposed without heat-shrink sleeving with adhesive lining.
608|||Letting bare wire protrude beyond the lug or crimping the insulation inside the lug.
609|||The fuse protects the cable
610|||The fuse is chosen by the current the cable can carry and is placed as close as possible to the source, that is the battery or the bus bar. Thickening the cable and enlarging the fuse increases the risk instead of protecting the cable.
611|||If a cable or lug is hot to the touch, its insulation has melted or the lug has blackened, switch that circuit off at the main switch and tell a technical service. These signs are the first warning of a fire risk.
612|||What cable size does a 10-amp load need in a 12-volt system?
613|||If the load is 6 metres away and you aim for a 3% voltage drop, about 6 mm² is needed. The size grows with longer distance or higher current; the exact value should be calculated.
614|||Can I solder the cable ends and connect them?
615|||No, solder alone is not recommended. Vibration breaks the soldered joint. Use a suitable lug, the right crimping tool and, where needed, adhesive-lined heat-shrink sleeving.

618|||An anode (zinc) is a metal that deliberately corrodes to protect valuable metals such as the propeller, shaft and engine. It is replaced when about half has dissolved; zinc or aluminium is preferred in salt water, magnesium in fresh water.
